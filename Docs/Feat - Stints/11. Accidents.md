## 2. Allocate Maximum 3 Skills

These skills are derived from the chapter's focus on spin recovery, accident prevention, and safety preparation.

1.  **Skill 1: Spin Recovery (Correction → Pause → Recovery)**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to execute the "Correction, Pause, Recovery" sequence when the rear end steps out, and to recognize when it's too late (90°+ yaw) and apply "Both Feet In."
    - **Feedback:** The "Yaw Rate Danger Gauge" and "Two Feet In" alert guide the driver through the recovery. Success is measured by saving the spin or safely stopping.
    - **Relevant Text:** *"If one turn of the steering wheel isn't enough, grab another handful and dial in more correction... The pause is your trigger to begin unwinding the opposite lock." (Page 170)*

2.  **Skill 2: Common Error Prevention**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to recognize and avoid the five most common errors that lead to accidents: Early Apex, TTO, Mismatched Downshift, Going Too Deep, and Stackup.
    - **Feedback:** The "Common Error Predictor" alerts the driver when they are approaching a dangerous pattern (e.g., turning in too early, snapping off the throttle).
    - **Relevant Text:** *"Spins and crashes are most often caused by mistakes and not equipment failures... you can use the following shopping list of common errors." (Page 172)*

3.  **Skill 3: Safety & Survival Mode**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to prepare for the worst—proper safety gear, understanding the "Both Feet In" rule, and knowing what to do after an impact.
    - **Feedback:** The "Safety Gear Checklist" ensures proper preparation. The "Accident Log" provides post-incident analysis to learn from mistakes.
    - **Relevant Text:** *"The best advice in preparing for the possibility of an accident is to outfit yourself and your racecar with the best safety equipment you can acquire." (Page 176)*

## 4. Suggest Suitable Car and a Track

- **Suggested Car:** **2014 BAC Mono**
    - **Rationale:** The chapter's spin recovery examples are best practiced in a lightweight, open-wheel car. The BAC Mono provides clear feedback on yaw and slip, and its lightweight construction means lower impact forces if a spin occurs. It's also a car that requires manual shifting, making the "mismatched downshift" error relevant.

- **Suggested Track:** **Sebring International Raceway (Full Circuit)**
    - **Rationale:** The chapter uses Sebring's **Turn 7** to illustrate early apex skid marks (Page 172) and **Turn 2** for the "jerking the car back on" error (Page 173). The long straights and tight corners provide a variety of scenarios for practicing spin recovery and avoiding common errors.

---

## 5. Suggest Game Type

- **Suggestion:** **Circuit Race (or Open Practice with Drivatars)**
- **Rationale:** The chapter discusses accidents in the context of racing—traffic, stackup, passing incidents. A Circuit Race with other cars provides realistic scenarios for practicing accident avoidance and the "Both Feet In" rule when incidents occur.

---

## 6. Time of Day

- **Suggestion:** **Late Morning (10:00 AM)**
- **Rationale:** The chapter mentions that oil and coolant are easier to spot "especially if the sun is low in the sky" (Page 175). Late morning provides clear visibility for spotting track debris, oil sheens, and flag signals from corner stations.

---

## 7. Weather

- **Suggestion:** **Clear**
- **Rationale:** The chapter's lessons on spin recovery and common errors assume a dry track. Rain introduces additional variables (hydroplaning, reduced grip) that complicate the core lessons of spin recovery and accident prevention.

---

## 8. Number of Laps

- **Suggestion:** **15 Laps**
- **Rationale:** With 15 laps, the driver experiences:
    - 5 laps for Stint 1 (Spin Recovery)
    - 5 laps for Stint 2 (Common Error Prevention)
    - 5 laps for Stint 3 (Safety & Survival)
    - Enough data for a meaningful "Accident Log" analysis.

---

## 9. Number of Drivatars

- **Suggestion:** **8-12 Drivatars**
- **Rationale:** The chapter discusses accidents involving traffic—stackup (Page 174), passing incidents (Page 175), and being the victim of someone else's error (Page 175). A moderate field provides realistic traffic without overwhelming chaos.
