## 1. Features, UI/UX HUD (The Oversteer/Understeer Analyst)

The HUD is built around the chapter's core physics: yaw angle, slip angle, rotation velocity, and the fluid nature of cornering balance.

### Core UI/UX Design Philosophy
- **The Spin Preventer:** The interface visualizes the car's rotation (yaw) so the driver can catch over-rotation *before* it becomes unrecoverable.
- **Physics Transparency:** Data is presented to show exactly *why* the car is sliding (e.g., "Understeer due to Unloaded Fronts").

## 2. Allocate Maximum 3 Skills

These skills are derived from the chapter's focus on fluid balance, oversteer/understeer correction, and rotation management.

1.  **Skill 1: Oversteer Recovery (Correction → Pause → Recovery)**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to instinctively execute the "Correction, Pause, Recovery" sequence when the rear steps out (via trailing throttle, power oversteer, or brake pop).
    - **Feedback:** The "Correction-Pause-Recovery Tracker" monitors if the driver waits for the "pause" (the eye of the storm) before unwinding the wheel. Success is measured by avoiding a counter-spin.
    - **Relevant Text:** *"Correction, pause, and recovery define the steering solution... transferring load with light throttle handles the footwork." (Page 64)*

2.  **Skill 2: Throttle Modulation for Exit Balance**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to use gradual throttle application to avoid "power oversteer" and to use a "breathe" (small lift) to cure understeer mid-corner.
    - **Feedback:** The system measures the rate of throttle change (RPM/sec). If it detects an abrupt application that causes rear slip angles to spike, it flags "Power Oversteer." If it detects a need for more steering lock due to early throttle, it flags "Understeer."
    - **Relevant Text:** *"Gradual applications of throttle tend to create understeer. Abrupt throttle applications... tend to create oversteer." (Page 61-62)*

3.  **Skill 3: Managing Understeer (The "Never Lift" Cure)**
    - **Focus:** Training the driver to resist adding more steering lock and instead "breathe" the throttle to transfer load to the front tires.
    - **Feedback:** If the car pushes wide (understeer) and the driver increases steering angle, a warning appears: "MORE STEERING = LESS GRIP". The correct action is to lift to 60-70% throttle to settle the nose.
    - **Relevant Text:** *"The answer is to lift off the throttle to keep from unloading the fronts as badly... a slight breathe is enough." (Page 68)*

## 4. Suggest Suitable Car and a Track

- **Suggested Car:** **2016 Dodge Viper ACR**
    - **Rationale:** This is a high-horsepower, rear-wheel-drive car. The chapter specifically discusses RWD dynamics (power oversteer, trailing throttle oversteer). Its power makes it easy to intentionally provoke slides for practicing the "Correction, Pause, Recovery" sequence in a safe (digital) environment. It perfectly illustrates the "King-Kong motor" scenario for oversteer.

- **Suggested Track:** **Sebring International Raceway**
    - **Rationale:** The chapter explicitly uses **Turn 7** (90-degree right), the **Chicane** (Turn 1 & 2), and the **Carousel** (Turn 9) as examples for understeer and oversteer correction (Pages 63, 67-68). Driving on the same track mentioned in the text provides immediate frame of reference for the theory.

## 5. Suggest Game Type

- **Suggestion:** **Circuit Race**
- **Rationale:** The chapter emphasizes "forget lap time and use each session as your own private clinic" (Page 70). A Circuit Race allows for repetitive corner practice to master the "fluid" car control movements (rotation, recovery) over multiple laps, rather than a one-off Timed Race which adds unnecessary pressure.

## 6. Time of Day

- **Suggestion:** **Late Morning (10:00 AM)**
- **Rationale:** The chapter heavily emphasizes "Using Your Eyes" and looking where you want to go (Page 71). Late morning provides strong visibility without harsh glare, reducing visual stress and allowing the driver to look *at the apex* and *track-out* while sliding, rather than looking at the barrier they fear hitting.

## 7. Weather

- **Suggestion:** **Clear**
- **Rationale:** The chapter's lessons on slip angle, yaw, and "threshold of grip" are built on dry pavement physics. Adding rain introduces a traction variable that changes the "Optimal Yaw Zone" significantly, which distracts from mastering the fundamental steering and throttle techniques described in the chapter.

## 8. Number of Laps

- **Suggestion:** **20 Laps**
- **Rationale:** The chapter notes that car control is *not* an inborn skill—it requires deliberate practice (Page 70). 20 laps allows for 3 distinct stints (approx. 6-7 laps each) to experiment with over-rotation, exit throttle, and understeer cures, with enough repetition to develop muscle memory.

## 9. Number of Drivatars

- **Suggestion:** **0 Drivatars**
- **Rationale:** The chapter warns against "emotional self-discipline" and getting distracted by competitors. The goal is to practice the "Correction, Pause, Recovery" and throttle modulation without the pressure of traffic, allowing full focus on the car's balance and rotation.

### Overall Summary & Next Steps

1.  **Top Priority:** **Patience in the Pause**. You are rushing the "Recovery" phase. You must wait for the car to settle.
2.  **Next Priority:** **The Gradual Squeeze**. Stop stomping on the throttle at the apex. Smoothness is speed, especially in a high-horsepower car.
3.  **Final Step:** **Trust the Lift**. If the car understeers, your instinct to add lock is wrong. Train yourself to **lift the throttle slightly** to shift weight to the front tires.

**Final Coaching Note:**
Remember, *"To be competitive, you have to be willing, even eager, to flirt with the car's limits. ... You find the limit by taking small bites."
